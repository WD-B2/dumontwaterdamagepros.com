What Kills Mold? Cleaning and Drying Facts for Dumont, NJ Homes

Killing mold is only half the job. Removing it and drying the area are what keep it from coming back.

By the Dumont Water Damage Pros team · November 12, 2026

Killing is not the same as removing

People search for what kills mold, and the honest answer has two parts. Some products reduce or kill growth on a surface. But dead mold is still material that needs to be removed, and a surface that stays wet will grow more.

That is why professionals talk about remediation, which means removing growth and fixing the moisture, instead of just killing it. Detergent and water, physical removal, and drying do more than any spray if the water source remains.

Common methods and their limits

Each method has a place and a limit.

Detergent and water with scrubbingHard, non-porous surfacesDoes not reach inside porous materials

Humidity is the quiet cause

Mold needs moisture, and the air itself can supply it. EPA guidance says to keep indoor humidity under 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent. A small hygrometer shows the number. A dehumidifier in a damp basement is often the best prevention.

Ventilation matters too. Bathroom and kitchen fans should vent outside, not into an attic. Clothes dryers should vent outside. Window condensation in winter is a sign that indoor air is too moist.

A short checklist before you reach for a spray bottle

These questions save time and money:

  • Is the source of water fixed and the area dry?
  • Is the surface hard and non-porous, such as tile, glass or sealed metal?
  • Is the patch small, about 10 square feet or less?
  • Are you wearing a respirator, gloves and eye protection?
  • Will the area be dried completely afterward?

If you answer no to any of these, call a professional or fix that step first. Our mold remediation page describes how crews handle larger jobs.

What does not work

Painting over mold with ordinary paint hides it for a while, but the paint peels as moisture continues. Sealing a wall with a stain-blocking primer helps only after the area is dry and clean. Fogging or spraying a scent over a musty smell does not remove growth.

Do not rely on air fresheners or ozone generators to solve a musty problem. If a room smells musty after a leak, the cause is probably moisture inside a wall, under flooring or in a crawl space. A moisture meter finds it, and drying fixes it.

Why porous materials are the hard case

Mold on tile or glass sits on the surface and can be scrubbed away. On drywall, wood framing, carpet and ceiling tile, the growth reaches into the material. Wiping the surface removes what you can see, but the roots stay behind and grow back when moisture returns.

That is why professionals remove porous materials that have visible growth, instead of treating them in place. The rule of thumb is simple. Clean what is hard and non-porous, and replace what is soft and porous. Frame lumber is in between, and it can often be cleaned and dried if it has not rotted.

A prevention plan for the next year

Most mold is prevented by keeping moisture in check:

  • Fix leaks as soon as you find them.
  • Keep humidity between 30 and 50 percent where you can.
  • Vent bathroom, kitchen and dryer exhausts outside.
  • Keep gutters clear and downspouts pointed away from the house.
  • Dry wet items within a day or two.
  • Check basements, crawl spaces and attics after heavy rain.

What to do if the smell persists

A musty smell after cleaning means moisture is still present somewhere. Look behind furniture, under carpet and inside closets on exterior walls. Check around plumbing, since a slow leak can wet a wall for months.

If you cannot find the source, a professional with a moisture meter or thermal camera can locate it. Finding the water is usually faster and cheaper than repeated cleaning.

A word on testing kits

Home mold test kits are sold in stores, and many owners ask whether to use them. A kit can show that spores are present, but spores are everywhere, so a positive result does not tell you how much is a problem. It also cannot tell you where the moisture is coming from.

If you see growth, you generally do not need a test to decide to fix the moisture and remove it. If you suspect hidden mold, a professional inspection with a moisture meter is more useful than a kit.

A short summary

Fix the water first, remove what cannot be cleaned and dry the area completely. Use detergent and water on hard surfaces, replace soaked porous materials and keep humidity in check. If the patch is large, hidden or caused by sewage or flood water, call a professional.

No product removes the need to find and fix the moisture. That is the step that decides whether the problem returns.

When sprays and fogs are sold to you

Some products promise to kill mold with a single spray or a fog treatment. Read the label and ask what happens to the moisture source and to the growth inside materials. A product that leaves the wall wet or the growth in place has not solved the problem.

Ask a contractor to explain any treatment in plain words, and ask what it does in addition to cleaning and drying.

Standards that guide mold work

Professionals follow ANSI/IICRC S520 for mold remediation, which is a published standard and not a product claim. It stresses finding the moisture source, containing the work and checking that the area is dry. Ask any company how its crews apply it.

Water is on the floor right now: do these four things first

If water is standing in your Dumont home, this is an emergency, and our line is open 24/7 for exactly this call. While you wait for a crew, find the source and stop it. For a pipe, that means the main shutoff valve, usually where the water line enters near the meter or in the basement.

Next, think about electricity. If water is near outlets, power strips, or the panel, stay out of the room and do not touch anything plugged in. Cut power at the panel only if you can reach it while standing on dry ground. If you cannot, call the electric utility or 911 before you call us.

Lift what you can off the wet floor, such as laundry, books, rugs and small furniture. Keep children and pets out. Then call, because water extraction is a race against the clock. Every hour a floor stays wet, more water wicks into baseboards, subfloor and the bottom of the walls.

What water extraction actually means

Water extraction is the physical removal of standing and pooled water from floors, carpet and low spaces. It comes before drying and before any repair. A crew uses submersible pumps for deep water, wheeled extraction units and wet vacuums for shallow water, and weighted extraction tools that pull water out of carpet and pad.

The goal is volume. A room can hold tens of gallons that no fan or dehumidifier can pull from the air quickly. Dehumidifiers handle moisture in the air and in materials, while extraction handles liquid water you can see. Skipping the first step makes the second take far longer.

Extraction is also a sorting step. The crew checks which materials can be saved by drying and which have soaked too long. That call is easier to make early, when pad and drywall have not yet wicked water upward.

Extraction in a Dumont basement, kitchen or ground floor

Dumont is a compact borough of about two square miles, and most calls involve the same few places. Finished basements collect water from a failed sump pump or a heavy storm. Kitchens and laundry rooms see appliance and supply line leaks. Second-floor leaks run down through ceilings into the rooms below.

Each place changes the method. In a basement, a pump pulls the water down gradually, because lowering it too fast can stress walls and floors. On a finished main floor, the crew works room by room so water does not travel under doors into dry rooms. If the leak started upstairs, extraction on the lower floor is usually paired with ceiling water damage repair once the area is dry.

What gets pulled out, and what gets decided on the spot

Extraction is not only a pump on a hose. A crew walks through and makes decisions that shape the rest of the job:

  • Carpet is extracted in place when it is clean water and the carpet is in good shape, using weighted wands.
  • Carpet pad is often removed and discarded, because it holds water like a sponge and dries slowly.
  • Baseboards may be pulled to let water drain from the wall bottom plate.
  • Furniture legs are lifted onto blocks so the floor beneath can be reached.
  • Wet contents are moved to dry rooms and photographed for your records.

When the standing water is gone, the work is not finished. The crew moves into structural drying and dehumidification so the materials, not just the surface, reach a safe moisture level.

What shapes the price of a water extraction job

No honest company can name a number before someone looks at the water. These are the factors that move the price:

  • The water category, since contaminated water needs protective gear and disposal steps.
  • How much of the floor is affected and how deep the water is.
  • Which materials are involved, such as carpet, pad, hardwood, tile or finished basement walls.
  • How long the water has been sitting before extraction starts.
  • Whether drying and repair follow, and for how many days equipment stays in place.

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered by homeowners policies, while gradual leaks and neglect often are not, and flood damage usually needs separate flood coverage. Documenting the loss while the water is still there

Take photos and short video before the crew moves anything, including the water line on walls, the source, and any affected belongings. Note the date and time you found it. If you can read the water meter, photograph it.

Keep damaged items you can safely store until your insurer says otherwise. Your photos and a plain list of what was affected make a claim easier to describe later.
